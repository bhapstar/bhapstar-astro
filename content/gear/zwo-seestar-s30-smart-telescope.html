The telescope that actually gets used

There is a gap between the setup that produces your best images and the setup that produces most of them. A full rig means a mount, tripod, cables, power, alignment and a controller. On a work night with thin cloud forecast, the honest answer is often that it is not worth it.

The S30 removes that calculation entirely. Thirty millimetres of aperture at 150mm focal length, f/5, with mount, camera, filter, storage and processing inside one body. Put it down, open the app, pick a target. It plate-solves, slews, tracks and stacks unaided.

What it delivers

A recognisable, shareable image of a large target in twenty minutes, which is a completely different proposition from nothing at all. It has more of the gallery to its name than its aperture suggests: Andromeda, Triangulum, the Pinwheel, the Leo Triplet and the Little Beehive are all its work, every one of them unfiltered.

The sensor is a 2.1 megapixel IMX662 and the aperture is 30mm, so detail is limited and heavy cropping is not on. Treat it as a wide-field deep-sky instrument and a fine Moon camera rather than a planetary one, where 30mm simply cannot resolve the detail regardless of software.

Who else it is for

Beginners, obviously. Less obviously, it suits people who already own a serious rig. It lowers the bar on marginal nights, and it can be handed to a family member without a briefing, which the Askar V on a harmonic mount decidedly cannot.

ZWO have replaced it with the S30 Pro, so new stock is limited. If you are choosing between the two, the aperture has not changed and the answer is less obvious than the names suggest.

Getting one now

ZWO have discontinued the model, so the used market is increasingly the route to one. That is not a bad thing: a sealed unit with no user-serviceable optics and no consumables is a lower-risk second-hand purchase than most astronomy gear. Check the battery health and that the charging port works, and there is not much else to go wrong.
