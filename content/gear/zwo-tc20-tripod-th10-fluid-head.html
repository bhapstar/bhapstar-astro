The part that arrived without being chosen

This came bundled with the Seestar S30 rather than bought deliberately, which is how most people end up with their first decent tripod. It is worth a page of its own anyway, because a tripod is the last thing people think about and often the first thing that limits them. One that flexes turns every gust into trailed stars, and no amount of guiding rescues a support that is moving.

The TC20 and TH10 work as a pair. The tripod provides a stable base, the head provides smooth damped movement on top of it. But filing the TH10 under "accessory" undersells it badly, because on a Seestar it is the part that unlocks equatorial mode.

Equatorial mode is the reason to own the TH10

In alt-az mode the S30 and S30 Pro track perfectly well. The problem is not tracking, it is that the field slowly rotates during the exposure. That puts a ceiling on sub length: 10, 20 or 30 seconds depending on where the target sits and how much corner trailing you will accept. Push past it and the stars at the frame edges smear into arcs while the ones at the centre stay round, which is the signature of rotation rather than bad tracking.

Tilt the whole unit over so its azimuth axis points at the celestial pole, roughly 25 degrees above the horizon from Dubai, and the rotation stops. The Seestar is then turning on one axis at sidereal rate, the way an equatorial mount does. Sub length goes up to 60 seconds with round stars right into the corners.

The TH10 is what makes that tilt practical rather than a fight. It has a graduated tilt scale, a bubble level, and fine geared adjustment that is damped rather than free, so a small correction holds where you put it instead of drifting back under the weight of the telescope. Any 3/8-16 head will bolt a Seestar on. Very few will let you dial in a latitude angle and trust it to stay there for three hours.

Sixty second subs are worth the setup. For the same total integration you stack fewer frames, so less read noise accumulates, less time is lost to readout between exposures, and faint signal in a nebula's outer structure has four to six times longer to build per frame than it does at 10 seconds. On a 30mm aperture working from a bright city, that difference is the difference between a target being reachable and not.

Worth knowing before you buy: ZWO list EQ mode as a headline feature of the S30 Pro and then note in smaller print that additional accessories are required. This head is that accessory. There is more on how it works in practice in the S30 Pro walkthrough.

What else a fluid head is for

Smooth continuous motion. That suits visual sweeping, terrestrial and wildlife use, framing a landscape with a smart telescope on top, and any video work. In practice this also carries a camera for wide-field and star-trail work, and a small refractor for visual use.

None of that makes it an equatorial mount. The TH10 has no motors and does not track anything. It is a wedge with a damping mechanism: it sets the angle and holds it, and the Seestar does the tracking from there. Put a camera or the Askar V on top instead and the tracking has to come from somewhere else, which is what the harmonic mount is for.

Judging a tripod

Two things decide whether one is genuinely stable: how quickly vibration damps out after you touch it, and whether the leg locks hold under load without creeping. Both are easy to test at home with the payload mounted, and both tell you more than a load rating does.

The carbon legs matter for a duller reason than load capacity. A Seestar is light, and light instruments are the ones that move when a breeze comes through or when someone walks past on a balcony. Stiffness here is not about carrying weight, it is about not passing vibration into a lens that is midway through a 60 second sub.
