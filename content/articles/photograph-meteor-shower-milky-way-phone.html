The short version

The camera in your pocket will photograph the Milky Way.

Not a smudge or a hint, but the bright core, the dust lanes and enough stars that people ask what you shot it with. Two things decide whether it works, and neither of them is the phone: something to hold it still, and a sky dark enough to be worth pointing at.

What you need

Almost nothing. A phone from the last few years, and the two things below. That is genuinely the whole list.

A tripod

Night photography works by holding the shutter open for several seconds, and anything you are holding in your hand moves during that time. A small tabletop tripod with a phone clamp costs very little and fits in a coat pocket.

There is a bonus that surprises people. Many phones will only offer their longest exposures once they are certain the phone is completely still. Put it on a tripod and the camera app quietly gives you settings you have never seen in it before.

A dark sky

This is the part worth planning around. From a city, the glow in the sky is brighter than the Milky Way itself, and no amount of editing recovers something the phone never saw. From a properly dark spot, the same phone in the same hands produces something you will want to print.

Dark is closer than most people assume. An hour or so out of most cities is usually enough, and the difference is dramatic rather than subtle. Check a light pollution map, head away from the nearest town, and give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust once you arrive. The Milky Way often becomes visible to the naked eye while you are still setting up.

When and where to look

The bright part of the Milky Way, called the core, is a summer object if you are in the northern hemisphere. From roughly April to September it rises in the south-east after dark and arcs across the southern sky, sitting highest around the middle of the night in midsummer.

Any free stargazing app will show you exactly where it is from where you are standing, on the night you are standing there. Hold the phone up, find the core, point the camera at that patch of sky.

Then give the frame something to stand on. A rock, a dune ridge, a tree line, or a friend looking up. A photograph of nothing but sky is a record; the same sky above something recognisable is a picture. Because you will be shooting from one spot for a while, two minutes spent on this at the start pays for the whole session.

Settings, by phone

Find the right mode and the phone does the rest. Here is where it lives on each kind.

iPhone

There is no dedicated astro mode, but the route is short. Open the camera and use the main wide lens rather than the ultra-wide or the zoom, because it has the biggest sensor and lets in the most light. Put the phone on the tripod. The moon icon appears automatically in the dark.

On models up to the iPhone 16, an exposure slider then runs out to around 30 seconds once the phone knows it is not being held. Drag it to the maximum and tap the shutter.

On the iPhone 17 series that slider is gone. Night mode is now three settings, Off, Auto and Max, reached from the moon icon or the camera controls button. Auto will not give you a long exposure however still the phone is, so choose Max. Take your hands off and wait a few seconds before checking the number, because touching the phone resets it.

If your model offers ProRAW, turn it on. It records a file you can brighten properly afterwards, rather than a finished picture that has already decided what the sky should look like.

Google Pixel

The easiest option on any phone. Open the camera, switch to Night Sight, put the phone on the tripod and leave it alone. After a moment the mode relabels itself as astrophotography and offers an exposure of up to about four minutes, quietly taking many frames and combining them for you. Point it, press the button, walk away.

Samsung Galaxy

The Pro mode in the normal camera gives you a manual shutter up to 30 seconds, which is plenty. But Samsung also has a proper astrophotography mode, and it is hidden.

It lives in Expert RAW, a free download from the Galaxy Store rather than something already installed. Open Expert RAW, tap the settings icon, and turn on Special photo options. An astrophotography icon then appears on the shooting screen. It only shows at 12MP resolution, so if you cannot find it, check that first. Samsung lists the mode as supported on the S22 and S23 series onwards.

Inside it you set how long to shoot for and the phone combines the frames itself. There is also a Sky Guide overlay that draws the constellations over the live preview, which is genuinely useful when you are standing in the dark trying to work out which way to point.

Other Android phones

Most have a Pro mode somewhere in the camera app, and 30 seconds is plenty. Several makers have added their own night modes worth hunting for. It is worth spending ten minutes going through every mode in your camera app before deciding your phone cannot do this, because the useful one is rarely on the front screen.

Two of those matter more than the rest. Focus is the step most people skip: autofocus has nothing to lock onto in the dark, so slide it all the way to infinity by hand. And push the ISO up until the sky looks grey rather than black on screen, then back off one step.

If there is a meteor shower on

Meteor showers happen on roughly the same dates every year, and a quick search will tell you what is coming up. The good news is that you barely have to change anything: the setup above already photographs them. A meteor lasts a fraction of a second, so it either crosses your frame while the shutter is open or it does not.

Three small adjustments help.

Keep the exposures short and repeat them. If you are using a four minute mode, drop to 20 or 30 seconds instead. A meteor that lands in a four minute exposure is competing with four minutes of skyglow; the same meteor in a 20 second frame stands out sharply.

Take a lot of frames. This is the whole trick. One exposure is a lottery ticket, thirty is a decent chance, and a couple of hours is how people come home with the shot. If your phone has a timelapse or interval option that keeps the individual frames, set it going and forget about it. If not, tapping the shutter each time the last frame finishes works perfectly well.

Point away from where the meteors come from. Every shower appears to radiate out from one patch of sky. Meteors near that point look like short stubs, while the long, dramatic ones appear well off to the side. So aim somewhere high and dark that is not directly at it.

The nice part is that nothing is wasted. Every one of those frames is also a Milky Way exposure, so on a quiet night for meteors you still go home with the galaxy.

A few things that make a real difference

  • Clean the lens. A fingerprint you would never notice by day turns every bright star into a smeared halo. Ten seconds with a cloth is the cheapest improvement available.
  • Use the main camera, not the ultra-wide. The ultra-wide is tempting because it covers more sky, but it has the smallest sensor and gathers the least light.
  • Turn your screen brightness down. Your eyes take about twenty minutes to adapt to the dark and a bright screen undoes that in a second, for you and for anyone sitting near you.
  • Bring a power bank. Long exposures drain a phone quickly, and a spare charge is the difference between stopping at midnight and staying for the best hours.
  • Watch for dew. On a humid night condensation creeps onto the lens gradually. If everything suddenly looks soft, give it a wipe and carry on.

Star trails, as a bonus

If you leave the phone running for an hour, you can also turn those frames into a star trail, where the stars draw curved lines as the Earth turns. Some Samsung phones have this built in: switch to Hyperlapse, set the speed to 300x, and a star trails toggle appears. Several other makers hide something similar in their own night modes.

Editing afterwards

Edit on the phone itself, which is more than good enough. Lift the shadows, pull the highlights down, add a little contrast, and lower the temperature slider until the sky looks neutral rather than orange. If you shot RAW there is far more room in the file than you expect, and the core usually appears with one gentle push of the shadows.

Keep it restrained. A slightly dark, clean sky looks better than a bright, noisy one.
