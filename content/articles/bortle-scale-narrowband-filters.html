What the scale measures

John Bortle published the scale in Sky & Telescope in February 2001. It runs from Class 1, the darkest sky left on Earth, to Class 9, an inner-city sky. What makes it useful is that it is written in terms of what you can actually see rather than a meter reading. Class 1 says the Milky Way casts shadows. Class 8 says you can read a newspaper outside.

Each class also maps onto two numbers. Naked-eye limiting magnitude (NELM) is the faintest star you can pick out without optics. Sky Quality Meter readings (SQM) are measured in magnitudes per square arcsecond, and because the magnitude system is logarithmic, a difference of one SQM point is roughly two and a half times the sky brightness.

Two things about the table are worth flagging. The SQM figures are the commonly quoted ranges rather than anything Bortle himself specified, and different sources shade the boundaries slightly differently. The NELM figures are also optimistic. A 2014 study found that Bortle overestimated how visible diffuse objects are to a typical observer, and that a limiting magnitude of 6.0 to 6.5 is a more realistic ceiling than the 7.1 the scale assigns to Class 2. Treat the numbers as a guide to roughly where you are, not a certificate.

Where I actually shoot

Most of what is on this site was shot from a balcony in Dubai. That is Bortle 8, sometimes 9 depending on which way the scope is pointed. The sky overhead is a flat grey-orange. On an average night I can see perhaps a dozen stars with the naked eye, and the Milky Way has never once been visible from there.

The other site is Al Quaa, about two hours inland toward the Empty Quarter. That is a Bortle 2 sky, and the difference is not incremental. The Milky Way comes up as a structured band with visible dust lanes, and the sky background in a sub-exposure drops by more than two orders of magnitude.

Driving out is the best possible fix for light pollution. It is also the one you cannot use on a Tuesday. The filters exist because the balcony is where most of the clear nights get used.

What light pollution does to an exposure

A common assumption is that a bright sky drowns out faint targets, in the sense that the target signal gets weaker. It does not. The photons from the nebula arrive at the sensor at exactly the same rate over Dubai as they do over Al Quaa. Nothing about the city changes what the nebula is doing.

What changes is everything arriving alongside them. Skyglow adds a large, even offset to every pixel, and that offset carries its own shot noise. Because shot noise scales with the square root of the signal, a background a hundred times brighter comes with ten times the noise. The nebula is still there in the data, sitting on top of a noisy floor that is far taller than it is. Subtracting the offset is trivial. Subtracting the noise it brought with it is impossible.

The practical effects stack up. Sub-exposures have to be shorter or the background clips. Stars bloat. Colour calibration gets harder because the offset is not neutral. And the total integration time needed for a given result goes up sharply, which is the part that decides whether a target is worth attempting at all.

Why a filter can help at all

The reason any of this is solvable comes down to a difference in spectra.

Emission nebulae glow because their gas is ionised by nearby hot stars, and ionised gas emits at specific wavelengths rather than across the spectrum. Hydrogen-alpha sits at 656.3nm in the deep red. Doubly ionised oxygen emits at 500.7nm and 495.9nm in the blue-green. Hydrogen-beta is at 486.1nm and ionised sulphur at 671.6nm. Nearly all the light from the Rosette or the Lobster arrives in those handful of narrow spikes.

Skyglow does not. Sodium and mercury street lighting produce a few strong lines that are easy to block, which is what the old light pollution filters were built for. LED lighting, which is what the Gulf is now lit with, produces a broad white continuum with a blue peak and a long phosphor tail. It covers the whole visible band. There is no line to notch out.

That mismatch is the whole opportunity. If the target emits in narrow lines and the sky emits everywhere, a filter that only opens two small windows throws away almost all the skyglow and keeps almost all the target.

The 7nm and 3nm filters

The Optolong L-eXtreme is the one that gets used most. It passes a 7nm band at hydrogen-alpha and a 7nm band at O III, and blocks essentially everything else. Out of the roughly 300nm of visible spectrum, it is open for 14nm of it.

The Svbony SV220 does the same job with 3nm windows. Halving the window width roughly halves the skyglow that gets through, while the emission lines themselves are narrow enough that most of the target signal still fits. The catch is that the line is not infinitely narrow, the filter's transmission curve has shoulders, and a tighter band is less forgiving of the light cone from a fast telescope hitting the coating off-axis. The Askar V at 495mm is f/6.2, which is slow enough that this is not a problem.

What this looks like in practice is longer subs. Under a filter that tight, a 300 second exposure from the balcony has a background level lower than a 30 second unfiltered one. The sensor is no longer racing to fill up with skyglow, so exposures can run long enough for the faint signal to climb clear of read noise. Total integration still has to be generous, because the filter is rejecting real photons too, but the hours now go somewhere useful.

Two side effects are worth knowing about. Bright stars can show halos, which is a coating artefact and varies between samples. And star colour goes strange, because the only star light getting through is whatever falls inside two narrow windows, so everything comes out magenta and teal. Both are fixable in processing, and the Siril workflow covers the star handling.

Where narrowband does nothing

Galaxies, globular clusters and reflection nebulae do not emit in lines. They shine by starlight, which is a continuum, or by scattering starlight, which is also a continuum. A dual narrowband filter cuts a galaxy by the same proportion it cuts the sky, so the contrast ratio is unchanged and all you have done is throw away most of your photons.

This is where the L-Quad Enhance comes in. It is a broadband filter with several wide passbands rather than two narrow ones, designed to notch out the worst of the artificial lines while leaving enough of the spectrum open that a galaxy still registers. It is a much gentler intervention, and correspondingly a much smaller improvement. Optolong do not recommend it above Bortle 7, and the balcony is Bortle 8, so it lives in the category of things that help a little rather than things that solve a problem.

What a filter cannot fix

A narrowband filter changes which photons reach the sensor. It does not change anything else about a bad site.

  • Gradients. Skyglow is not uniform across the frame. One horizon is brighter than the other, and the filter reduces that gradient without flattening it. Background extraction still has work to do.
  • Transparency. Dust and humidity scatter the light you want as readily as the light you do not. In the Gulf, summer haze costs more than the city does.
  • Seeing. Turbulence sets how small a star can be. No filter affects it.
  • Continuum targets. Covered above, and it is the main reason a Bortle 8 gallery ends up heavy on nebulae.

The honest summary is that a 3nm dual narrowband filter over a Bortle 8 sky gets emission nebulae to roughly where an unfiltered camera would get them from a Bortle 4 sky, and does nothing at all for anything else. That is a large win in a narrow lane. Every image on this site of a nebula was made in that lane. Every galaxy was made in the car.
