Why an imager owns eyepieces at all

An imaging rig never shows you the sky. It shows you a screen. That is fine for producing photographs and slightly beside the point on a clear night when someone asks what you are looking at.

This kit stays in the bag for two reasons. The first is practical: putting an eyepiece in and finding a target by eye is sometimes quicker than waiting for a plate solve, particularly on the Moon and planets. The second is that handing someone a telescope they can actually look through is a completely different experience from showing them a stacked image afterwards.

What kits like this are good at

They are an efficient way to cover a range of magnifications without buying premium eyepieces one at a time. For occasional visual use alongside an imaging setup, that is the right trade. The colour and neutral density filters are most useful on the Moon, which is bright enough to be genuinely uncomfortable at low magnification.

What they are not

Bundled eyepieces are rarely the equal of good individual ones. Expect narrower apparent fields, more edge softness and less comfortable eye relief than a dedicated wide-field eyepiece. If visual observing is your main interest, two good eyepieces will outperform a kit of eight.

The nebula filters supplied in kits of this type also tend to be modest performers. They are not comparable to a proper narrowband imaging filter, and under a bright sky the visual improvement is small.

Who it suits

Imagers who want a visual option without much investment, and anyone introducing other people to a telescope. If you are a serious visual observer, buy individual eyepieces instead.
