Narrower, and what that actually buys

The SV220 does the same job as a 7nm dual narrowband filter, with tighter windows: 3nm at hydrogen-alpha and 3nm at doubly-ionised oxygen. The logic is straightforward. If skyglow is broadband and the target is not, then narrowing the window rejects proportionally more sky while keeping the same signal.

In principle that means better contrast from a bright sky. In practice there are two complications that make 3nm the more demanding choice rather than the automatically better one.

Complication one: signal

A narrower passband transmits less total light, so exposures lengthen and total integration has to grow. If your limiting factor is available hours rather than sky brightness, a 3nm filter can produce a worse result than a 7nm filter in the same session, simply because you gathered less.

Complication two: focal ratio

Interference filters are specified for light arriving perpendicular to the surface. In a fast optical system the light cone is steep, rays strike at an angle, and the effective passband shifts towards shorter wavelengths. The narrower the nominal band, the more damage a given shift does, because there is less margin before the emission line falls outside the window entirely.

This is why 3nm filters are usually recommended for f/5 and slower, and why the same filter can perform well on one telescope and disappoint on a faster one.

The comparison worth making

You also own the Optolong L-eXtreme at 7nm, which makes a direct comparison possible: same telescope, same camera, same target, matched integration time. That is the test almost nobody publishes, because it requires owning both.

Who it suits

Imagers with slower optics, patience for long integrations, and skies bright enough that the extra rejection earns its cost. If you image at f/4, or if your sessions are short, a 7nm filter is the more sensible starting point.
