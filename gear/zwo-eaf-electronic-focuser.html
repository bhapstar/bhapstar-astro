Focus does not stay where you put it

Telescope tubes expand and contract as the temperature falls through the night, and the optical path changes with them. A tube in focus at nine in the evening is not in focus at two in the morning.

How much that matters depends on the focal ratioThe focal length divided by the width of the lens, written like f/5.8 and often called the f-number. Smaller numbers gather light faster, so you need less time on the target, but focus becomes more critical., and it matters more than most people expect. The usable focus zone scales with the square of the f-number, so an f/5 instrument tolerates roughly a quarter the error of an f/10 one. At the fast end you are working within a few tens of microns, comfortably less than a night's thermal drift.

What it does about it

The EAF replaces the focuser knob with a stepper motor driven from the ASIAIR. It runs an autofocus routine, stepping through positions and measuring star sizes to find best focus without anyone touching the telescope, and it refocuses during the session, either at intervals or when the temperature has moved by a set amount.

The second is the one that changes results. Refocusing periodically keeps the whole night's subsOne single exposure out of the many that get stacked together. Sixty subs of one minute each make one hour of data. usable instead of accumulating a drift that quietly ruins the last third of them. It also removes a source of vibration, since focusing by hand means touching the telescope and waiting for it to settle, several times a night.
