Photographing the Milky Way and Meteor Showers with Your Phone

No camera and no experience needed, just a phone, a small tripod and a dark sky. How to photograph the Milky Way and catch meteors on the same night, with the settings to use on an iPhone or an Android.

Your phone is better at this than you think

The camera in your pocket will photograph the Milky Way. Not a smudge, not a hint, but the real thing: the bright core, the dust lanes running through it, and enough stars to make people ask what you shot it with. Phone sensors have got good enough over the last few years that this has quietly stopped being a trick and started being something anyone can do on a clear night.

Meteors are the more exciting half of the same evening, and they work a little differently. A meteor lasts a fraction of a second, so it either lands in the frame while the shutter is open or it does not. The bright ones record beautifully, and the Perseids in August throw out plenty of those, including the occasional fireball that draws an audible reaction from everybody watching. The approach is simple: set the phone running and let it collect, then spend the night actually looking up. Whatever the phone catches is a gift on top of the evening you already had.

The happy accident is that both subjects want exactly the same setup. Point the phone at the Milky Way, leave it firing, and any meteor that crosses the frame lands in a picture you were taking anyway.

Two things worth getting right

Almost everything else on this page is optional. These two are what turn a quiet night into a good one, and both are easy.

A small tripod

Every night mode on every phone works by holding the shutter open for seconds at a time, so the phone needs to be still while it does that. A little tabletop tripod with a phone clamp costs very little, fits in a coat pocket and is genuinely all this needs. It is the single best thing you can spend money on for night photography, by a wide margin.

There is a bonus that surprises people. Many phones only unlock their longest exposures once the gyroscope confirms the phone is completely still, so a tripod does not merely sharpen the picture, it hands you a better mode than you can otherwise reach. Put the phone on a tripod and the camera app offers you options you have never seen in it before.

A dark sky, for the Milky Way

For meteors you can get away with a lot, because a bright fireball punches through most skies. The Milky Way is different, and this is the part worth planning around: it needs a genuinely dark site. From a city balcony the sky glowThe glow of that scattered artificial light coming back down at you. It is what makes a city sky look washed out rather than black. is brighter than the galaxy, and no amount of clever processing recovers something the sensor never saw. From a properly dark site, the same phone in the same hands produces something you will want to print.

The good news is that dark is closer than people assume. An hour or so out of most cities is usually enough to make the difference, and the change is dramatic rather than subtle. Check a light pollutionArtificial light from streets and buildings that scatters in the air and brightens the whole night sky, hiding everything faint. map, pick a direction away from the nearest town, and give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust once you arrive. The Milky Way often becomes visible to the naked eye while you are still setting up, which is the moment most people remember from their first proper dark-sky night.

On an iPhone

There is no dedicated astro mode, but the path to a good frame is short. Open the camera, use the main wide lens rather than the ultra-wide or the zoom, since it has the biggest sensor and the fastest apertureThe size of the opening the light comes through, written as an f number such as f/2.8. Confusingly, a smaller f number means a bigger opening and more light., and put the phone on the tripod. The moon icon appears automatically in the dark, and on models up to the iPhone 16 the exposure slider then runs out to around 30 seconds once the phone knows it is not being held. Drag it to the maximum and tap the shutter.

On the iPhone 17 series that slider is gone. Night mode is now three settings, Off, Auto or Max, reached from the moon icon or from the camera controls button (the grid of six dots). Auto will not give you the long exposure however still the phone is, so choose Max: 10 seconds handheld, up to 30 on a tripod. Take your hands off and wait a few seconds before you check the number, because tripod detection is a live judgement and touching the phone resets it.

Turn on ProRAW if your model offers it. It gives you a file with real latitude to brighten later, rather than a processed JPEG that has already decided what the sky should look like. For the Milky Way in particular, this is where a lot of the detail comes from.

The bigger step up is a third-party manual camera app. Several expose ISOHow strongly the camera amplifies the light it has collected. A higher number makes a dark scene look brighter, but it also makes the picture grainier, so you use the lowest number that still shows the sky., shutter and manual focus directly, and at least one has a mode built specifically for meteors that watches the frame and keeps only the exposures where something changed. That is exactly the right tool for this job, and it costs less than a coffee.

On an Android

If you have a Pixel, you already have the best automatic option on any phone. Open the camera, switch to Night Sight, put the phone on the tripod and leave it alone. After a moment the mode relabels itself as astrophotography and offers an exposure of up to about four minutes, taking a long series of frames and stackingCombining many frames of the same patch of sky into one. The random grain is different in every frame so it averages away, while the real detail is in the same place every time and stays. them internally. For the Milky Way this is close to magic. Point it, press the button, and walk away.

Samsung: there is a real astro mode, but it is hidden

The Pro mode in the standard camera gives you manual shutter up to 30 seconds along with manual ISO and focus, which is enough for both subjects. What most people miss is that Samsung also ships a dedicated astrophotography mode, and it is buried two menus deep.

It lives in Expert RAWThe camera's unprocessed file, holding everything the sensor recorded. A JPEG throws most of that away, and you need it when you come to brighten a very dark picture., a free download from the Galaxy Store rather than something preinstalled. Open Expert RAW, tap the settings icon, and turn on Special photo options. An astrophotography icon then appears on the shooting screen alongside a multiple exposure mode. It only shows up at 12MP resolution, so if you cannot find it, check that first. Samsung lists astro mode as supported on the S22 and S23 series onwards, with Expert RAW itself running on the S20 and later.

Inside astro mode you set the shooting duration and control focus and white balanceThe setting that decides whether the picture looks warm and orange or cool and blue. Setting it by hand keeps hundreds of night frames matching each other. yourself, and the phone stacks the frames for you. There is also a Sky Guide overlay that draws the constellations over the live preview, which is genuinely useful for framing when you are standing in the dark trying to work out where Cygnus is.

Star trails, hiding in the hyperlapse menu

Samsung also has a star trailsA picture where the stars are drawn as long arcs rather than points, made by leaving the shutter open, or by stacking a long run of frames end to end. mode, and it is somewhere nobody would think to look: the Hyperlapse mode in the normal camera app. Switch to Hyperlapse, set the speed to 300x (it is marked with a small star icon), and a star trails toggle appears at the bottom of the screen. Switch the resolution to UHD while you are there. About an hour of recording gives you a video roughly twelve seconds long, and you can pull stills out of it afterwards.

Two warnings. It needs a tripod and a charged phone, because an hour of recording will flatten a battery. And support is patchy across the range: it turned up on the S22 and S23 series and the Z Fold4, but people on mid-range handsets have found the toggle does nothing.

Everything else

Most other Android phones have a Pro mode somewhere in the camera app, and 30 seconds is plenty for both subjects. Several makers have gone further than that and added their own night modes worth hunting for. My Honor Magic V6 has a light painting mode with a star trails option inside it, which does much the same job as the Samsung hyperlapse trick. Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo all ship something comparable under one name or another. It is worth spending ten minutes going through every mode in your camera app before you decide your phone cannot do this, because the useful one is rarely on the front screen.

Manual settings, if your phone offers them

Finding the Milky Way, and framing it

The bright part of the Milky Way, the core, is a summer object for the northern hemisphere. From roughly April to September it rises in the south-east after dark and arcs across the southern sky, sitting highest around the middle of the night in mid-summer. August evenings are close to ideal, which is one of the reasons Perseid nights are such good value: the meteors and the galaxy are out together.

Any free stargazing app will show you exactly where it sits from where you are standing, on the night you are standing there. Hold the phone up, find the core, and point the camera at that patch of sky.

Then give the frame something to stand on. A rock, a dune ridge, a tree line or a friend looking up turns a record of the sky into a photograph, and it gives a viewer a sense of scale that pure sky never does. Because you are shooting from one fixed spot for a while, the two minutes you spend on composition at the start pay for the whole session.

Keep it firing

This is the trick behind almost every meteor picture you have seen. One exposure is a lottery ticket, thirty is a decent chance, and a couple of hours is how people come home with the shot. If your phone has a timelapseHundreds of still frames played back one after another as a short video, so hours of sky movement pass in seconds. or interval option that keeps the individual frames, set it going and forget about it. If not, tapping the shutter each time the last frame finishes works perfectly well and costs nothing but a little attention.

The same run of frames does double duty, which is the nicest part. Every one of them is a Milky Way exposure, so even on a quiet night for meteors you go home with the galaxy. Stack them later and you have a cleaner image than any single frame, or line them up in order and you have a timelapse of the sky turning.

Small things that make a real difference

  • Clean the lens. A fingerprint you would never notice by day turns every bright star into a smeared halo at night. Ten seconds with a cloth before you start is the cheapest improvement available.
  • Use the main camera, not the ultra-wide. The ultra-wide is tempting because it covers more sky, but it has the smallest sensor and the slowest aperture. The main lens gives a cleaner, brighter result.
  • Focus to infinity by hand if you can. AutofocusA programmed routine through an Electronic Automatic Focuser that refocuses the telescope as the temperature drifts through the night, or at set timed intervals. has little to lock onto in the dark. If your app offers a manual focus slider, push it all the way and leave it there.
  • Screen brightness down, warm colours on. Your eyes take about twenty minutes to fully adapt, and a bright screen resets that in a second, for you and for anyone sitting near you. Red torch light protects it.
  • Bring a power bank. Long exposures and screen-on time drain a phone quickly, and a spare charge is the difference between stopping at midnight and staying for the best hours.
  • Watch for dewWater that condenses out of the air onto cold glass overnight and quietly fogs everything. A small heater strip or a cloth hood keeps it off.. On a humid night, condensation creeps onto the lens gradually. If everything suddenly looks soft, give it a wipe and carry on.

Afterwards

Edit on the phone itself, which is more than good enough. Lift the shadows, pull the highlights down, add a little contrast, and lower the temperature slider until the sky reads neutral rather than orange. If you shot RAW there is far more room in the file than you expect, and the Milky Way coreThe bright, crowded middle of our own galaxy, seen edge on. From the northern half of the world it sits low in the south in summer. usually comes up with one gentle push of the shadows. Keep it restrained: a slightly dark, clean sky looks better than a bright, noisy one.
