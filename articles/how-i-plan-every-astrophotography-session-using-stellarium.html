How I Plan Every Astrophotography Session Using Stellarium

The software I open before every session. What is up tonight, how high it gets, when it is highest, and whether it will actually fit inside the frame.

The short version

The clear night is not the scarce thing. The planning is.

Stellarium shows you the sky from your own balcony, on any date, at any time, running as fast or as slow as you like. Ten minutes with it tells you what is up tonight, how high it gets, when it is highest, whether the Moon is going to ruin it, and whether the thing you want will even fit inside your camera frame. All of that happens indoors, in the warm, before anything comes out of the bag.

Most wasted nights are lost before you go outside

Here is the pattern. The forecast is clear, you get everything set up, you polar alignLining a mount's main axis up with the point the sky turns around. Get it right and one slow, steady turn follows the stars all night., you pick a target, and forty minutes later you realise it is sitting twelve degrees above the rooftops and going down. Or it fills half your frame with nothing left for the edges. Or the Moon comes up at midnight and washes out the second half of the session.

None of that is a skill problem. It is a planning problem, and every part of it is knowable in advance.

I open Stellarium before every session. Not sometimes. Before every one, whether I am shooting from the balcony in Dubai or driving out to Al Quaa. It is the first thing I open and it is the reason I know what the night is going to look like before I have carried anything downstairs.

What it actually is

Stellarium is planetarium softwareA program that draws the sky as it appears from a chosen place at a chosen time, and lets you run time forwards and backwards. Used for working out what is visible before going outside.. You tell it where you are, you give it a date and a time, and it draws the sky exactly as it will appear above you. Not an artist's impression. The real positions.

The part that makes it a planning tool rather than a pretty picture is that you can move time. Wind forward an hour and watch your target climb. Wind forward to next Tuesday. Wind forward to February and see what winter brings round. The sky in the software behaves the way the real one does, so anything you can work out in it will hold up outside.

Start with the free web version

If you have never used it, do not install anything yet. Open Stellarium Web in a browser, put in your town, and have a look around. It costs nothing and there is nothing to set up.

Even now I use it for quick questions, usually on a laptop that is not mine. It answers the ordinary ones perfectly well:

  • What is up tonight?
  • When does Orion clear the buildings?
  • Where is Saturn?
  • Which constellations belong to this month?
  • When does proper darkness begin?

That last one catches people out. Sunset is not darkness. There is a long gap afterwards while the sky is still bright enough to spoil an exposure, and astronomical darknessThe part of the night when the Sun is far enough below the horizon that it adds no light to the sky at all. It starts well after sunset and is the only time the faintest things can be photographed properly., the point where it stops mattering, arrives later than most people assume. How much later changes through the year and with how far north or south you are.

The five things I check before anything is packed

This is the whole routine, and it takes about ten minutes.

What is visible. Not what is technically above the horizon, but what is high enough to be worth pointing at.

How high it gets. The altitudeHow high something is above the horizon, measured in degrees. Zero is the horizon and 90 is straight overhead. The higher it is, the less air you are looking through. a target reaches decides how much atmosphere you are shooting through. Low down you are looking through a much thicker, dirtier slice of air, and from a city that slice is full of light.

When it is highest. Every object reaches a highest point and then starts back down. That moment is culminationThe moment an object reaches its highest point in the sky before it starts back down. It is the best time to photograph something, because that is when you are looking through the least air., and it is the middle of your best window.

What the Moon is doing. Phase and position, both. A bright Moon on the other side of the sky is survivable. A bright Moon forty degrees from your target is not, at least not without a narrowbandA filter that only lets through the few exact colours that glowing gas clouds give off, and blocks nearly everything else. Streetlight is mostly blocked, the nebula is not. filter, which is a whole subject of its own that I have written about in the piece on Bortle skies and narrowband filters.

How long I realistically have. Between the sky going properly dark, the target clearing the obstruction, and the target dropping too low again, there is a window. Sometimes it is five hours. From a balcony with a building on one side, sometimes it is ninety minutes, and knowing that changes what is worth attempting.

Framing is the part worth paying for

Everything above is available free. This next part is not, and it is the reason I stopped guessing.

The software can draw your actual camera and telescope onto the sky as a rectangle. Give it your focal lengthHow much a lens or telescope magnifies, measured in millimetres. A small number gives a wide view of the sky, a large number a narrow, closer one. and your sensor size and it works out the field of viewHow much sky fits inside one frame. A wide field shows a whole constellation, a narrow one a single small galaxy. for that pairing, then shows you, to scale, precisely what will land on the chip. No arithmetic, no comparing angular sizes on a web page and hoping.

Before I had this I framed by trial and error, which on a target that only clears the rooftops for two hours means finding out you were wrong on the one night you had.

Worth being clear about what costs money, because the naming is confusing. Stellarium on a computer is free and open source, and it has this feature built in. The phone app is a different product with a free tier, and the framing tool is one of the things behind the paid Plus unlock. I use the phone version, so I paid. If you work at a desk, you do not have to.

Build the equipment library once

The setup is the boring bit and it is worth doing properly. Sit down for half an hour and enter every combination you own. After that it is permanent.

Mine currently holds nine. The Askar V is a modular scope, so it accounts for six of them on its own: the reducerAn extra lens that shortens the focal length, giving a wider view and gathering light faster. The picture covers more sky and needs less time., native and extenderThe opposite of a reducer: an extra lens that lengthens the focal length, so you get a closer view of a smaller patch of sky. configurations, each at 60mm and 80mm of apertureThe size of the opening the light comes through, written as an f number such as f/2.8. Confusingly, a smaller f number means a bigger opening and more light.. Then the two Seestar units, and the Samyang 135mm for wide work. Every one of those rectangles is drawn for the sensor in the ASI585MC Air, because the frame is a product of the scope and the chip together. Change the camera and every rectangle changes with it.

Once they exist, the question changes. It stops being "will this fit" and becomes "which of these nine frames it best", and that is a question you can answer by tapping through them while the kettle boils.

The target picks the telescope, not the other way round

Deep-skyAnything beyond our own solar system: nebulae, galaxies and star clusters. Faint, far away, and the reason for long exposures. objects vary in apparent sizeHow big something looks from Earth, rather than how big it really is. Measured as an angle, so the Moon and the Sun have almost the same apparent size even though one is vastly larger. far more than people expect. Andromeda is six times the width of the full Moon. The Ring Nebula is about a twentieth of that width. There is no focal length that suits both, and no amount of skill at the telescope rescues a target that does not fit.

Take the Crescent Nebula. I can lay my 270mm frame over it, then 384mm with the reducer, then 500mm, then 600mm, then the Seestar, then the 135mm lens, and see all six compositions inside a minute. One of them will have the nebula sitting properly in the frame with room to breathe. That is the one I take out.

The same process works in reverse when a scope is already set up and I want to know what suits it tonight.

Planning around a balcony

City imaging adds a constraint the software handles better than I expected. My balcony faces one way. There is a building to one side and a wall on the other. Plenty of the sky simply is not available to me, and plenty more is only available between certain hours.

Because Stellarium knows my latitude and can run time forward, I can find the exact window when something crosses the gap I can actually shoot through. That turns a frustrating site into a workable one. It is also how I know when a target is worth waiting three weeks for, because three weeks earlier in the evening puts it somewhere different.

For the dark site trips out to Al Quaa it does a different job. Those nights are long and there is no obstruction, so the plan becomes a running order: this one while it is high, that one after it culminates, the wide field once the Milky Way coreThe bright, crowded middle of our own galaxy, seen edge on. From the northern half of the world it sits low in the south in summer. is up. Nothing is decided in the dark at two in the morning, which is when I make my worst decisions.

It has taught me the sky, which I did not expect

The unplanned benefit is that I now know where things are. Not from memorising a chart, but from watching the same sky wheel round the same way a few hundred times at ten thousand times normal speed.

I know Orion is a winter target and that Cygnus owns the summer. I know roughly where to look for Andromeda without checking. That knowledge makes everything downstream faster, and it is the sort of thing that is much easier to absorb by playing with a model than by reading about it. If you want the underlying framework rather than the software, I have written about how the sky is laid out and why it moves the way it does.

Is the paid version worth it

For learning the sky and for casual use, no. The free options are complete and I would happily recommend them to anyone who never intends to photograph anything.

For photography, the framing tool alone repaid the cost on the first night it stopped me packing the wrong scope. That is a low bar for a piece of software that costs less than a filter, and it is worth checking before you spend, because on a computer that feature is free.

The summary is that Stellarium has changed my hit rate more than any single piece of hardware I have bought. Not because it makes the pictures better, but because it stops me spending clear nights on targets that were never going to work. Given how few genuinely good nights there are in a year here, that turns out to matter more than almost anything else in the rest of the setup.
