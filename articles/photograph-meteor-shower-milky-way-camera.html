How to Photograph the Milky Way with a Camera

The short version

One wide lens on a tripod, pointed at a dark sky, is the whole technique.

Set the camera to manual, focus on a bright star, and take the same frame over and over for an hour or two. From roughly April to September the bright core of the Milky Way is up after dark. If a meteor shower happens to be on, the same frames catch that too.

What you need

A camera that shoots manual and RAWThe camera's unprocessed file, holding everything the sensor recorded. A JPEG throws most of that away, and you need it when you come to brighten a very dark picture., the widest and fastest lens you own, and a tripod that will not sag. That is the real list.

Lens choice matters more than the camera body. Wide is better, because it covers more sky and lets you fit the Milky Way above a landscape. Fast is better, meaning a lens whose smallest f-numberThe focal length divided by the width of the lens, written like f/5.8 and often called the f-number. Smaller numbers gather light faster, so you need less time on the target, but focus becomes more critical. is low, because it gathers more light in the time available. Something around 14mm to 24mm at f/2.8 is close to ideal, and inexpensive in manual-focus form.

If your widest lens only opens to f/4, that is fine. You will need a higher ISOHow strongly the camera amplifies the light it has collected. A higher number makes a dark scene look brighter, but it also makes the picture grainier, so you use the lowest number that still shows the sky. to compensate, and the result will be a little noisier, but the picture still works. Use the lowest f-number your lens has, whatever that number is. There is more on the bodies and lenses I use over on the gear pages.

The rest is small: a spare battery or two, a remote releaseA cable or wireless button that fires the shutter for you. Pressing the camera itself shakes it, and at these exposure lengths that is enough to smear the stars. or intervalometerA timer that presses the camera's shutter over and over on its own, so you can take hundreds of frames without touching it. Many cameras have one built into the menus, and if yours does not, a small plug-in remote does the same job. that can be locked on, and a cloth for the dewWater that condenses out of the air onto cold glass overnight and quietly fogs everything. A small heater strip or a cloth hood keeps it off..

A dark site changes everything

This is the single decision that matters most. Under city glow the background sky is brighter than the Milky Way, and no amount of processing brings back what the sensor never recorded. Drive an hour out to a properly dark site and the same camera, the same lens and the same settings produce a completely different photograph.

A light pollutionArtificial light from streets and buildings that scatters in the air and brightens the whole night sky, hiding everything faint. map and a little planning is all it takes, and it is the highest-value hour you will spend on the whole night.

How long can the shutter stay open before stars start trailing?

The Earth turns, so stars drift across the frame. Leave the shutter open too long and they stop being points and become short dashes. Wider lenses tolerate longer exposures.

There are two common rules of thumb. The older 500 rule is generous; the newer NPF ruleA formula for the longest exposure you can use before stars stop being points and start being short lines. It takes account of the lens, the aperture and the size of the pixels, so it is stricter than the older 500 rule. is stricter and holds up better on a modern sensor. Composition

A frame of nothing but sky is a technical record. Put something along the bottom of it, a tree line, a dune ridge, a building edge, or a person looking up, and it becomes a photograph.

Spend five minutes on this before you start, because you will be shooting hundreds of frames from one fixed position and the composition you choose at the beginning is the one you keep. In summer, framing towards the south puts the Milky Way coreThe bright, crowded middle of our own galaxy, seen edge on. From the northern half of the world it sits low in the south in summer. in the scene.

Then start the intervalometer and leave the camera to it. There is nothing to watch and nothing to adjust. Lie back and enjoy the sky while it collects.

If there is a meteor shower on

Meteor showers arrive on roughly the same dates every year, and a quick search will tell you what is next. Almost nothing changes: the setup above is already the right one. But three things are worth knowing.

Point away from the radiant. Every shower appears to stream out from one point in the sky. Meteors near that point are travelling towards you and look like short stubs. The long, photogenic ones are always 40 to 60 degrees away from it. Aim somewhere high and dark, off to one side.

Stay late. Rates almost always build through the night. Early on, the radiant is still low and much of the shower is happening below the horizon. By the small hours the Earth has rotated to face into the stream. If you can only stay out for part of the night, stay late rather than arriving early.

Keep the exposures shortish. A meteor lasts a fraction of a second, so what matters is how much of the night your shutter is actually open and how many separate frames you end up with. The shutter speeds in the table above are already right. Just do not be tempted to lengthen them.

Expect most frames to contain nothing. That is completely normal. Across a few hours a handful will have a meteor in them, and one or two of those will be keepers. Meanwhile every single frame is still a Milky Way exposure.

Afterwards

You will come home with several hundred near-identical frames. That is a rich set of material rather than a chore, and there are three good things to do with it.

Combine them into one cleaner Milky Way image. Averaging many frames together is what removes the grainy noise, and the difference is large.

Stack the whole run into a star trailA picture where the stars are drawn as long arcs rather than points, made by leaving the shutter open, or by stacking a long run of frames end to end., where the stars draw curved lines as the Earth turns. A free second image from files you already have.

Pick the best meteor frame and process it on its own. You can also combine several meteors onto one sky, which is completely legitimate as long as you say that is what you did.

All of it processes happily in Siril, and if all you have on the night is the phone in your pocket, that works too.
